\begin{abstract}
When a linear probe recovers information about a \emph{future} token from a
language model's hidden state, it is tempting to read this as evidence that the
model is \emph{planning} ahead. We show that such probe accuracies are
\emph{baseline-dependent} and conflate two distinct sources: positional
structure already present at initialization, and computation the model
\emph{learns} during training. We introduce a per-position \emph{staircase}
baseline that compares a target-position probe against the strongest earlier
position, and a \emph{training-dynamics decomposition} that splits the resulting
gap into a positional \emph{floor} (measured at random initialization) and a
\emph{learned} component (acquired during training). Applying this diagnostic to
19 models across nine architecture families and five tasks (116 probing
experiments), we find that a \emph{single} methodology produces three
qualitatively different signatures: a large learned component for rhyme
(a planning-consistent learned signal in a domain where prior causal work found
planning), a flat positional floor for code return-types (an artifact that
vanishes under a mean-pool baseline and is unchanged by training), and an absent
signal for a controlled question-answering task. The pattern is consistent
across three model scales (1.4B--6.9B) and across all probed layers, not a
single lucky layer. A within-model checkpoint analysis shows that the rhyme
probe gap and behavioral rhyme generation emerge together during training
(Spearman $\rho=0.75$, $p=0.03$), while a cross-model comparison deliberately
does \emph{not} claim the gap ranks unrelated models. We argue that probing
claims about planning should be reported against position baselines and, where
checkpoints exist, decomposed against the positional floor---and we provide a
concrete reporting checklist.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Linear probes are a standard tool for asking what information a language model's
hidden states contain \citep{alain2017,belinkov2022,tenney2019}. A recent and
striking application probes for information about \emph{future} tokens: if a
hidden state at position $t$ already encodes a property of a token the model has
not yet generated, this is read as evidence that the model is \emph{planning}
ahead rather than predicting one token at a time. Causal steering work supports
this interpretation in some settings: \citet{maar2026} show that steering vectors
applied at intermediate positions can manipulate future rhymes and question
answers in models from 1B parameters upward.

Consider a concrete case. A model reads the first line of a couplet ending in
\emph{``light''} and is about to generate a second line that must rhyme. A probe
at the position just before the rhyming word predicts the rhyme family well
above chance. Is the model \emph{planning} the rhyme, or was the rhyme
constraint already recoverable from \emph{``light''} several tokens earlier?
The raw probe accuracy cannot tell these apart. Probing classifiers can exploit
confounds and recover information that is not \emph{used} by the model
\citep{hewitt2019,belinkov2022,voita2020}, and the distinction matters precisely
because probing is far cheaper to scale than causal intervention---making it
tempting to treat probe accuracy as a proxy for mechanistic evidence. The
central question of this paper is methodological: \emph{when a probe recovers
future-token information, how much of that signal reflects model computation,
versus information already recoverable from earlier token positions?}

We argue that this question has three distinct quantities, only the first of
which is usually reported: (i) the raw target-position probe accuracy;
(ii) the \emph{gap} between that accuracy and the strongest earlier position,
which removes information already present upstream; and (iii) the \emph{learned}
component of that gap, isolated by subtracting the gap measured at random
initialization. A signal can have high raw accuracy, a large gap, and yet a
\emph{near-zero learned component}---in which case the probe is reading
positional structure, not acquired planning. We make three contributions.

\textbf{(1) A per-position staircase baseline.} For each task we compare the
target-position probe against the \emph{single strongest earlier position}
(Section~\ref{sec:staircase}), a stricter baseline than the mean-pooled
``signature region'' baseline used in prior work. This already changes the
picture: tasks that look like planning under weak baselines often do not survive
a per-position comparison.

\textbf{(2) A training-dynamics decomposition.} Probing a model at random
initialization isolates the part of any staircase gap that is purely
\emph{positional}---it requires no learning. Subtracting this \emph{floor} from
the trained model's gap yields the \emph{learned} component
(Section~\ref{sec:decomp}). To our knowledge this is a novel way to separate
positional artifacts from acquired computation in future-token probing.

\textbf{(3) A large-scale, multi-architecture evaluation.} We apply the
diagnostic to 19 models spanning nine architecture families, five tasks, and
(for three Pythia scales) eight training checkpoints---116 probing experiments
with linear and MLP probes, two baselines, full layer curves, name controls,
logit-lens corroboration, and behavioral validation.

The result is not a single ``planning'' verdict but a \emph{discriminating
diagnostic}. The same methodology yields three qualitatively different
signatures (Figure~\ref{fig:regimes}): rhyme shows a large gap that
\emph{grows} during training and persists under every baseline; code return-type
prediction shows a small gap that is \emph{flat} across training and
\emph{collapses} under a mean-pool baseline; and a controlled neutral-QA task
shows \emph{no} gap at any training stage. The diagnostic's value is that it
tells these cases apart---a method that returned ``planning'' for all of them
would be useless. We close with a concrete checklist of what future-token
probing studies should report.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\paragraph{Probing and its controls.} Linear probing
\citep{alain2017,conneau2018} tests what representations encode, but
\citet{hewitt2019} showed that probe accuracy conflates information in the
representation with the probe's own capacity, motivating \emph{control tasks} and
selectivity. Information-theoretic and minimum-description-length formulations
\citep{voita2020,pimentel2020} make the same point: absolute accuracy is not the
right unit. Our staircase is in this tradition---it replaces an absolute accuracy
with a difference against a position-matched baseline, and the training-dynamics
floor plays a role analogous to a control task but derived from the model itself
rather than from random labels.

\paragraph{Future-token information and planning.} A growing literature asks
whether models represent future tokens. Future Lens \citep{pal2023} decodes
future tokens from individual hidden states; \citet{maar2026} provide causal
steering evidence for implicit planning in rhyme and question answering; and
work on emergent world representations probes for state that the model later
acts on \citep{li2023,gurnee2024}. Our work is complementary and deliberately
diagnostic: rather than adding another planning claim, we ask how much of a
probing signal would survive a stricter baseline and how much is acquired during
training.

\paragraph{Reading hidden states across layers.} The logit lens
\citep{nostalgebraist2020} and tuned lens \citep{belrose2023} read hidden states
through the unembedding; layerwise probing shows that information is distributed
across depth \citep{tenney2019}, and feed-forward layers act as key--value stores
\citep{geva2021}. We use the logit lens as an independent, classifier-free check
on one of our tasks (Section~\ref{sec:robust}).

\paragraph{Training dynamics and model development.} Capabilities and internal
structure emerge at identifiable points in training: induction heads
\citep{olsson2022}, grokking and progress measures \citep{nanda2023}, and
knowledge acquisition in game-playing agents \citep{mcgrath2022}. The Pythia
suite \citep{biderman2023} releases intermediate checkpoints that make such
analysis possible. We use checkpoints not to track loss but to define a
positional floor and to correlate representational change with behavioral change.

\section{Method}
\label{sec:method}

\subsection{The per-position staircase}
\label{sec:staircase}

Each task provides prompts $x$ with a categorical label $y$ describing a
\emph{future} token (e.g.\ the rhyme family of an upcoming line-ending word). For
a model with hidden state $h^{(\ell)}_p(x)$ at layer $\ell$ and position $p$, and
a designated \emph{target} position $t(x)$, we train a probe at the target and at
every earlier position $p<t$. Target positions are defined by interpretable
\emph{resolvers}---e.g.\ the newline before the upcoming line, the last word of
the first line, or the question mark---and we report results for each resolver
rather than hand-picking one (Section~\ref{sec:probing}). Earlier positions are
all token positions strictly before the target in the same prompt; because
prompts vary in length, positions are indexed from the target backward so that
``earlier'' is well defined across examples.

Writing $\mathrm{acc}^{(\ell)}_p$ for the cross-validated accuracy of a probe at
layer $\ell$, position $p$, the \emph{staircase gap} at layer $\ell$ is
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:gap}
\Delta^{(\ell)} \;=\; \mathrm{acc}^{(\ell)}_{t}
\;-\; \max_{p < t}\, \mathrm{acc}^{(\ell)}_{p}.
\end{equation}
The baseline is the \emph{single strongest} earlier position, not an average: if
any earlier position already carries the label, $\Delta$ is small or negative. A
positive gap is necessary (not sufficient) evidence that the target position adds
future-token information beyond what is already linearly recoverable earlier.

\paragraph{Layer selection and non-max summaries.} We report the \emph{headline
gap} as the largest-magnitude $\Delta^{(\ell)}$ across probed layers and
resolvers, with a 500-sample bootstrap confidence interval. Because this is a
maximum over layers and resolvers, it is susceptible to selection inflation. We
therefore also report \emph{non-max} summaries---the mean and median gap over all
probed layers, and the mean over the final third of layers---and show the full
per-layer curves (Section~\ref{sec:layers}, Appendix~\ref{app:nonmax}). The
qualitative ordering of tasks is preserved under every summary, which is the
property the headline statistic alone cannot guarantee.

\subsection{Dual baselines}
\label{sec:dual}

The max-earlier baseline of Eq.~\ref{eq:gap} compares single positions. As a
second, independent baseline we probe the \emph{mean-pooled} activations over all
positions up to the signature region (the ``$N{+}P$'' baseline of prior work),
giving a gap $\Delta_{\text{mp}}$. A signal that reflects genuine target-position
computation should survive both baselines; a positional artifact may survive the
single-position comparison yet collapse against a pooled representation that
aggregates the same information from many earlier tokens.

\subsection{Training-dynamics decomposition}
\label{sec:decomp}

A staircase gap at a \emph{trained} model mixes two things: structure present
before any learning (token identity in the embedding, positional encoding) and
computation acquired during training. We separate them using checkpoints. Let
$\Delta_{0}$ be the headline gap at random initialization (step~0) and
$\Delta_{\text{final}}$ the gap at the end of training. We define
\begin{align}
\text{floor} &= \Delta_{0}, &
\text{learned} &= \Delta_{\text{final}} - \Delta_{0}.
\end{align}
The floor is the gap attributable purely to position and embedding; the learned
component is what training adds. A large learned component is the signature we
associate with an acquired, planning-consistent signal; a near-zero learned
component (regardless of the raw gap) indicates a positional artifact. We test
significance of the learned component with an example-level permutation test, but
emphasize \emph{magnitude} over significance: with hundreds of examples a $+2$pp
effect can be ``significant'' yet behaviorally negligible
(Section~\ref{sec:dynamics}).

\subsection{Probing setup}
\label{sec:probing}

Probes are logistic-regression classifiers on PCA-reduced activations
(128 components by default). To avoid leakage, \emph{PCA is fit inside each
training fold} and applied to the held-out fold; cross-validation is stratified
by label. For the neutral-QA task, where prompts form minimal pairs that share
surface text, we use \emph{grouped} cross-validation
(\texttt{StratifiedGroupKFold}) and a cluster bootstrap so that paired prompts
never straddle the train/test split; ungrouped splitting inflates accuracy by
letting the probe memorize pair identity (Appendix~\ref{app:grouped}). We fit
two-layer MLP probes on a subset of models to test whether findings are artifacts
of probe linearity (Section~\ref{sec:robust}). For every task we report a
bag-of-words baseline to flag cases where the label is trivially recoverable from
surface tokens; all hyperparameters (PCA dimension, probe regularization, fold
count, bootstrap size) are fixed across tasks and models before analysis.

\section{Tasks and Experimental Setup}
\label{sec:setup}

\subsection{Task construction}
\label{sec:tasks}

We study five tasks chosen to span the space from a planning-positive domain to
trivial surface prediction (Table~\ref{tab:tasks}). For each we specify the
target position, the label, the confound a naive probe might exploit, and how we
control it.

\textbf{Rhyme} (10 classes, 200 examples) uses the couplet data of
\citet{maar2026}. A prompt is the first line plus the start of the second; the
label is the rhyme family of the upcoming line-final word, and the target
position is the newline / last-word position where \citet{maar2026} found
steering to causally change the produced rhyme. The potential confound is that
the first line's final word already constrains the rhyme; the staircase controls
for exactly this by comparing against the strongest earlier position, and the
behavioral check (Section~\ref{sec:behav}) scores generated rhymes with a
pronunciation dictionary (out-of-vocabulary words fall back to a suffix match).

\textbf{Code} (5 classes, 507 examples) is Python return-type prediction. The
prompt is a function signature and body; the label is the return type and the
target is the position before the return annotation. The signature---name and
parameters---carries strong return-type cues, so we expect earlier positions to
match the target. We additionally stress-test this with opaque and adversarial
function names (Section~\ref{sec:robust}).

\textbf{QA-neutral} (2 classes, 161 examples) uses minimal question pairs that
share \emph{identical} surface text across the two answers (the same question
stem appears under both labels), so the label cannot be read off earlier tokens.
It is our cleanest test for a target-position signal in the absence of surface
confounds; minimal-pair members are kept in the same CV fold.

\textbf{QA-suggestive} (2 classes, 145 examples) uses questions whose surface
content predicts the answer (a weak-gap control), and \textbf{Trivia}
(5 classes, 100 examples) uses questions whose content fully determines the
answer category, saturating a bag-of-words baseline and making the staircase
uninformative by construction (a null we include to show the diagnostic does not
manufacture signal).

\subsection{Models}
\label{sec:models}

We evaluate 19 models spanning nine architecture families: Gemma-2
(2B/2B-it/9B/27B), Qwen3 (1.7B/8B), Pythia (410M--6.9B), GPT-2
(small/medium/XL), Mistral-7B, Llama-3.1-8B, Llama-3.2-3B, Falcon3-7B, OLMo-7B,
and StableLM-2-1.6B, covering absolute, rotary, and RoPE-with-normalization
positional schemes. For the training-dynamics decomposition we use the three
deduplicated Pythia scales (1.4B, 2.8B, 6.9B) for which intermediate checkpoints
are released \citep{biderman2023}, probing at steps
$\{0, 512, 4\mathrm{k}, 16\mathrm{k}, 32\mathrm{k}, 64\mathrm{k},
128\mathrm{k}, 143\mathrm{k}\}$.

\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

\subsection{Three diagnostic regimes}
\label{sec:regimes}

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.92\columnwidth]{fig6_regimes.pdf}
\caption{The training-dynamics decomposition places each task in one of three
regimes. Rhyme has a high positional floor \emph{and} a large learned component
(planning-consistent); code has a moderate floor but a near-zero learned
component (positional artifact); QA-neutral has neither (genuine null).
Pythia-1.4B.}
\label{fig:regimes}
\end{figure}

Our central result is summarized in Figure~\ref{fig:regimes}: decomposing each
task's staircase gap into a positional floor (gap at initialization) and a
learned component (gain over training) separates the tasks into three regimes.
Rhyme sits in the high-floor, high-learned corner; code has a moderate floor but
essentially no learned component; QA-neutral has neither. The remainder of this
section establishes each axis of this plot and shows it is robust to baseline
choice, layer selection, probe family, scale, and architecture.

\subsection{The gap is baseline-dependent and varies by an order of magnitude}
\label{sec:res-cross}

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{fig2_cross_model.pdf}
\caption{Per-position staircase gap by task, one point per model, colored by
architecture family; black bars are task means. Rhyme ($+64$pp mean) separates
from code and QA-suggestive ($\sim$$+12$pp), QA-neutral ($\sim$$0$), and trivia
($0$). The ordering holds across all nine families.}
\label{fig:cross}
\end{figure}

Figure~\ref{fig:cross} shows the headline gap for every (model, task) pair. The
gap varies by an order of magnitude across tasks and is remarkably consistent
within a task across architectures. Rhyme yields a large positive gap on all 19
models (mean $+63.6$pp, range $[+48.5, +76.5]$); code and QA-suggestive yield
small positive gaps (means $+12.0$ and $+12.7$pp); QA-neutral yields a small
\emph{negative} gap (mean $-1.2$pp, range $[-1.9,-0.6]$); and trivia yields
exactly zero, its label already saturated by bag-of-words. A paired Wilcoxon test
across models confirms rhyme exceeds code (median difference $+53$pp, $n{=}18$,
$p<10^{-4}$) and QA-neutral (median $+72$pp, $n{=}12$, $p<10^{-3}$).

The methodological point is that the \emph{same} measurement, applied uniformly,
places three future-token tasks (code, QA-suggestive, QA-neutral) near or below
the floor despite their being exactly the kind of task for which raw probe
accuracy is high. For code, raw target-position accuracy exceeds 90\% on most
models---but the gap over earlier positions is only $\sim$$12$pp, and (next) most
of that is positional. A positive probe accuracy is not a positive gap.

\subsection{The gap is broad across layers, not one lucky layer}
\label{sec:layers}

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{fig7_layer_curves.pdf}
\caption{Per-layer target accuracy (solid) and strongest-earlier accuracy
(dashed) for Gemma-2-2B. The rhyme gap is large at \emph{every} layer; the code
gap is small but consistent; QA-neutral shows target and earlier overlapping at
chance throughout. The headline result is not an artifact of selecting one layer.}
\label{fig:layers}
\end{figure}

Because the headline gap is a maximum over layers and resolvers, we examine the
full per-layer curves (Figure~\ref{fig:layers}). For rhyme the gap is large at
\emph{every} probed layer (target $\approx$ 80--100\% vs.\ earlier
$\approx$ 25--30\%); for code it is small but consistent; for QA-neutral target
and earlier accuracies track each other at chance across the entire depth. The
non-max summaries confirm this: averaging the gap over all layers (or over the
final third) leaves rhyme at $+46$ to $+75$pp, code at $+6$ to $+11$pp, and
QA-neutral at $\approx 0$ across the anchor models we examined
(Appendix~\ref{app:nonmax}). The task ordering---and the rhyme/code/QA-neutral
regime assignment---is therefore not a selection artifact of the maximum.

\subsection{Training dynamics decompose the gap}
\label{sec:dynamics}

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{fig1_training_dynamics.pdf}
\caption{Staircase gap across training (Pythia-1.4B, log-scale steps). Rhyme
starts at a $+34$pp floor and \emph{grows} to $+58$pp; code is \emph{flat} near
$+11$pp; QA-neutral sits at zero at every checkpoint.}
\label{fig:dynamics}
\end{figure}

Probing across checkpoints reveals \emph{why} the tasks differ
(Figure~\ref{fig:dynamics}). At random initialization, rhyme already shows a
$+34$pp gap---a substantial positional floor, since rhyme labels correlate with
token identity the embedding exposes---but the gap then \emph{grows} to $+58$pp:
training adds $+23.5$pp of learned signal. Code starts at a $+10$pp floor and
stays flat (learned $+2$pp): training adds essentially nothing at the target
position. QA-neutral sits at zero at every checkpoint, step~0 included.

An example-level permutation test finds the learned component significant for
both rhyme and code on Pythia-1.4B and 2.8B ($p<10^{-3}$ for rhyme;
$p<10^{-3}$ and $p{=}9{\times}10^{-4}$ for code). We stress that the
\emph{magnitudes}, not the $p$-values, carry the message: a $+2$--$4$pp learned
gap for code versus $+19$--$26$pp for rhyme. The decomposition---not statistical
significance---is what distinguishes acquired computation from a stable
positional artifact.

\subsection{A mean-pool baseline confirms the code gap is positional}
\label{sec:res-dual}

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.82\columnwidth]{fig4_dual_baseline.pdf}
\caption{Each point is a model: staircase gap under the max-earlier baseline
($x$) versus the mean-pool baseline ($y$). Rhyme (blue) persists under both
($+59$ to $+86$pp); code (orange) collapses to the $x$-axis ($-5$ to $+7$pp).}
\label{fig:dual}
\end{figure}

The training-dynamics view is corroborated by the second baseline
(Figure~\ref{fig:dual}). Under the mean-pool baseline the code gap collapses to
$[-5.1, +7.1]$pp---pooling earlier positions recovers as much return-type
information as the target position---while the rhyme gap remains large
($[+59.5, +86.5]$pp). Two independent baselines (a stricter single-position
comparison through training, and a pooled comparison) thus agree: the code signal
is recoverable from earlier positions, and the rhyme signal is not.

\subsection{Behavioral validation: representation tracks generation within a
model}
\label{sec:behav}

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{fig5_behavioral.pdf}
\caption{Within-model behavioral validation (Pythia-1.4B). The rhyme staircase
gap (blue) and the model's actual rhyme-generation accuracy (orange) rise
together; both emerge between steps 4k--32k. Within-model Spearman $\rho=0.75$
($p=0.03$), Pearson $r=0.88$ ($p=0.004$), $n=8$.}
\label{fig:behav}
\end{figure}

If the rhyme gap reflects an acquired, planning-consistent signal, it should
track the model's actual ability to produce rhymes. We measure behavioral
rhyme-generation accuracy (does the model complete a couplet with a correctly
rhyming word, scored with a pronunciation dictionary) at each Pythia-1.4B
checkpoint and compare it to the probe gap (Figure~\ref{fig:behav}). The two
emerge together: at initialization both are at floor (0\% generation, $+34$pp
probe), and both rise sharply between steps 4k and 32k. The within-model
correlation is strong (Spearman $\rho=0.75$, $p=0.03$; Pearson $r=0.88$,
$p=0.004$; $n=8$).

We report an important negative result and treat it as a feature, not a
weakness. The \emph{cross-model} correlation between probe gap and generation
accuracy is \emph{not} significant ($\rho=0.17$, $p=0.52$, $n=17$): a larger
probe gap in model $A$ than model $B$ does not imply $A$ generates better rhymes.
This is expected---generation quality depends on decoding, tokenization, and
scale, not only on whether information is linearly present---and it is precisely
why the within-model, across-training comparison (which holds architecture,
tokenizer, and decoding fixed) is the appropriate test. The staircase gap is a
diagnostic of \emph{representation}, which is necessary but not sufficient for
behavior; it is not a universal score for ranking unrelated models, and we do not
use it as one.

\subsection{Robustness: probe family, names, and the logit lens}
\label{sec:robust}

\paragraph{MLP probes.} The findings are not artifacts of linear probing. We
refit the rhyme and QA-neutral staircases with two-layer MLP probes on six models
across four families; in all 13 runs the MLP agrees with the linear probe in sign
and regime (large positive for rhyme, near-zero for QA-neutral;
Appendix~\ref{app:mlp}). Architecture coverage is broad: the rhyme gap is $+48$ to
$+77$pp on all nine families and is size-invariant from 0.12B to 27B parameters
(Appendix~\ref{app:size}).

\paragraph{Code name controls.} If the code signal is carried by earlier
signature positions, then (i) removing informative function names should not hurt
and (ii) \emph{misleading} names should hurt. Both hold. Replacing names with
opaque placeholders (\texttt{func\_000}) leaves probe accuracy essentially
unchanged (Pythia-2.8B: $0.906\rightarrow0.907$; Llama-3.2-1B:
$0.940\rightarrow0.812$), showing names are not necessary---parameter context
suffices. \emph{Adversarial} names that suggest the wrong return type crash
accuracy to near chance (Llama-3.2-1B: $0.386$). The probe is reading
earlier-position cues, consistent with the positional-artifact interpretation
(Appendix~\ref{app:names}).

\paragraph{Logit-lens corroboration.}
As a classifier-free check on the code domain, we read each hidden state through
the model's own unembedding (the logit lens) and measure how often the correct
return-type token is recoverable. This accessibility grows over training
(Pythia-2.8B: $0.23\rightarrow0.65$; Pythia-410M: $0.21\rightarrow0.61$ from step
0 to 143k), confirming that the model does learn to \emph{represent} return
types. Crucially, this does not contradict the flat code staircase: the logit
lens measures whether the information is present \emph{anywhere}, while the
staircase measures whether it is \emph{localized to the target position} beyond
earlier positions. The two together sharpen the paper's point---a growing
representation (logit lens up) need not imply target-position planning (staircase
flat) (Appendix~\ref{app:logit}).

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\paragraph{What the staircase does and does not show.} The staircase is a
diagnostic for \emph{evidential strength}, not a claim that a capability is
present or absent. A near-zero or negative gap means the probe has not
demonstrated target-position information beyond what earlier positions provide;
it does not prove the model lacks the relevant computation, nor that causal
methods would fail to find it. A large, baseline-robust, training-acquired gap
(as for rhyme) is the strongest \emph{observational} signature one can obtain
short of intervention, and it is consistent with the causal planning that
\citet{maar2026} establish for that domain---but our probes do not themselves
establish causation.

\paragraph{QA-neutral is not a refutation of Maar et al.} Our QA-neutral null
does not contradict prior causal QA results. QA-neutral is a deliberately
stricter, surface-controlled diagnostic: minimal pairs share identical text, so
\emph{any} earlier-position signal is removed by construction. A null here shows
that the staircase does not manufacture a positive gap merely because a task
involves a future answer; it does not claim that question-answering planning is
absent in the less controlled settings where steering succeeds.

\paragraph{Why the negative tasks are the contribution.} A reader might object
that only rhyme shows a large learned gap. We view the negative tasks as the core
of the contribution: they show the methodology \emph{discriminates}. The same
uniform measurement yields a growing-with-training, baseline-robust signal for
rhyme; a flat, pool-collapsible signal for code; and a flat-zero signal for
QA-neutral. A diagnostic that returned ``planning'' for everything would be
useless; the value is in telling these apart.

\paragraph{A checklist for future-token probing.} We recommend that studies
claiming future-token planning report: (1) raw target-position accuracy;
(2) the gap against the \emph{strongest earlier position}; (3) a second
\emph{mean-pool} baseline; (4) where checkpoints exist, the step-0 positional
\emph{floor}; (5) the \emph{learned} component (final $-$ floor); (6) full layer
curves or non-max summaries rather than a single maxed layer; (7) leakage checks
and grouped splits for any minimal-pair data; and (8) behavioral or causal
validation where feasible---reported \emph{within} a model across training rather
than across unrelated models. These are inexpensive additions that materially
change which signals count as evidence for planning.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

We introduced a per-position staircase baseline and a training-dynamics
decomposition for diagnosing probing claims about future tokens, and applied them
at scale across 19 models, nine architecture families, and five tasks. The
diagnostic cleanly separates an acquired, planning-consistent signal (rhyme: a
large learned component that grows with training, survives two baselines and all
layers, and tracks behavioral generation within a model) from a positional
artifact (code: a flat floor that collapses under pooling and is unmoved by
training) and a genuine null (QA-neutral). The message is methodological: probe
accuracy alone does not establish planning; the appropriate evidence is a
positive gap against a strong position baseline, ideally decomposed against the
positional floor measured at initialization.

\section*{Limitations}

Our diagnostic is observational: a positive staircase gap is necessary but not
sufficient evidence for planning, and a null gap does not establish that the
underlying computation is absent. The causal claim for rhyme rests on prior
steering results \citep{maar2026}, not on our probes. The training-dynamics
decomposition requires released intermediate checkpoints and is therefore
demonstrated on the Pythia suite only; we cannot decompose Gemma, Qwen, or Llama,
for which checkpoints are unavailable. The behavioral validation is within-model
across training for a single model scale; the cross-model correlation is not
significant, and we explicitly do not claim the probe gap predicts behavior
across architectures. The QA-suggestive and trivia tasks have fewer models and,
for trivia, a bag-of-words--saturated label that makes the staircase
uninformative by construction. The logit-lens corroboration is available for the
code domain only. Finally, our probes use PCA-reduced features at a fixed default
dimensionality; while the MLP results argue against a linearity artifact, we did
not run a full PCA-dimension or random-seed sensitivity sweep, and we report this
as an open item rather than claiming robustness we did not measure.

\section*{Ethics Statement}

This work analyzes publicly available pretrained language models on synthetic and
benchmark linguistic tasks. It introduces a diagnostic method and does not release
new models or data beyond derived probing results. We see no direct risk of harm;
the main societal relevance is methodological caution against overinterpreting
probing results as evidence of model capabilities.

\section{Grouped cross-validation for QA-neutral}
\label{app:grouped}

The QA-neutral task pairs each prompt with a minimal-pair counterpart that shares
identical surface text but a different answer. Ungrouped cross-validation lets the
two members of a pair fall on opposite sides of the train/test split, allowing the
probe to memorize pair identity and inflating accuracy. Switching to
\texttt{StratifiedGroupKFold} (keeping pairs together) with a cluster bootstrap
removes this leakage: the bag-of-words baseline moves to chance ($\approx0.51$)
and the headline gap moves to $\approx-1.2$pp. All QA-neutral numbers in the main
text use grouped CV.
